\section{Overview}
\label{sec:results_overview}

This chapter evaluates the Block-Insure prototype against the research questions
defined in Chapter~\ref{chap:methodology}. The evaluation covers smart-contract
correctness, code coverage, deployability constraints, gas efficiency of Merkle
commitment, synthetic fraud-risk classification, and exploratory local
claim-submission throughput. The auditor-reputation study is also reported as an
implemented but currently unmeasurable study because no finalized voting
sessions exist.

The results must be interpreted within the experimental boundary. The
blockchain is a local Hardhat network, healthcare records are deterministic and
synthetic, both oracle workers share prototype infrastructure, and the
throughput study consists of one local batch at each concurrency level.
Consequently, the results demonstrate implemented behavior and reproducibility;
they do not establish production performance, public-chain deployability, or
real-world fraud-model generalization.

\section{Gas-Efficiency Study}
\label{sec:gas_results}

\subsection{Protocol}

The gas study compared two first-write operations on a fresh
\texttt{GasTestContract}: storing \(N\) individual \texttt{bytes32} hashes in a
mapping and storing one \texttt{bytes32} Merkle root. Receipt gas includes
transaction execution and calldata for the tested operation. The independent
variable was
\begin{equation}
N\in\{10,25,50,100,200,500\}.
\end{equation}
For each \(N\),
\begin{equation}
Saving(\%)=
\frac{Gas_{\mathrm{individual}}-Gas_{\mathrm{root}}}
{Gas_{\mathrm{individual}}}\times100.
\label{eq:gas_saving_result}
\end{equation}

prototype verifies proofs off-chain.

\label{sec:risk_results}

\subsection{Dataset and Evaluation Protocol}

The deterministic synthetic registry contains 120 source records. After stable
sorting by hospital and invoice, the deterministic 80/20 split produced 96
training records and 24 held-out records. Scenario generation produced 130
training claim scenarios and 35 held-out claim scenarios. The held-out scenario
set contains 14 fraud cases and 21 legitimate cases.

Model likelihoods and anomaly statistics were calculated only from the training

not used for threshold selection.

\subsection{Interpretation}

The results support RQ3 only within the synthetic experiment. The training and
held-out protocol avoids direct test-label leakage during parameter training and
threshold selection. However, the data-generation rules strongly couple fraud
labels to observable statuses and signals. Evaluation queries are also
constructed from the same registry records, so ordinary legitimate cases are
exact matches. Furthermore, the runtime rule-based oracle comparison directly
checks the synthetic \texttt{fraudLabel}, although that label is excluded from
the Bayesian evidence factors. The measured AUC, AP, false positives, and false
negatives therefore describe behavior on designed scenarios, not expected
performance on noisy or adversarial real insurance data.

\section{Claim-Submission Throughput Study}
\label{sec:throughput_results}

\subsection{Protocol and Measurement Boundary}

The exploratory benchmark tested
\begin{equation}
C\in\{1,5,10,20,50\}
\end{equation}
concurrent claim submissions. Before each timed batch, the script purchased one
policy per claim; setup time was excluded. Every claim used unique document and
invoice hashes. The script measured:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item response time of the backend \texttt{/health} endpoint;
    \item blockchain \texttt{submitClaim} send-to-receipt time;
    \item combined end-to-end time; and
    \item successful-claim throughput based on batch wall-clock duration.
\end{enumerate}

The backend measurement represents basic API responsiveness, not the latency of
a backend claim-processing endpoint. All blockchain submissions used one signer
managed by an ethers.js \texttt{NonceManager}. Each concurrency level was run
once; therefore, the results are exploratory rather than statistically stable.

Throughput increased rapidly up to concurrency 20, where all submissions
succeeded at 48.3827 claims/s. Increasing concurrency to 50 produced only a
small throughput gain, to 49.2880 successful claims/s, while success rate fell
to 78\%. All 11 failures reported ``nonce too high'' because local Hardhat
automining does not queue out-of-order transactions. The result therefore
identifies a local signer/automining saturation point, not a smart-contract
validation limit.

Blockchain confirmation dominated the measured end-to-end duration. Depending
on concurrency, average blockchain time represented approximately
82.43\%--92.03\% of average end-to-end time, while the health-endpoint response
represented approximately 1.77\%--5.91\%. This supports RQ4's local conclusion
that transaction processing, rather than basic backend responsiveness, is the
primary measured bottleneck.

\section{Auditor-Reputation Study Status}
\label{sec:auditor_result_status}

The implemented analysis groups finalized votes by auditor, calculates
historical agreement with finalized consensus, reads current on-chain
reputation, and computes Pearson correlation:
\begin{equation}
r=
\frac{\sum_i(x_i-\bar{x})(y_i-\bar{y})}
{\sqrt{\sum_i(x_i-\bar{x})^2\sum_i(y_i-\bar{y})^2}}.
\label{eq:auditor_pearson_result}
\end{equation}
At least two auditors and nonzero variation are required. No finalized voting
sessions existed at the time of evaluation; consequently, no defensible
correlation can be calculated. RQ5 therefore remains unanswered. Agreement with
consensus would in any case be only a proxy for correctness because consensus
itself is not independent ground truth.

\section{Summary}

The evaluation demonstrates that Block-Insure implements and tests a substantial
claim-processing lifecycle, produces compact and highly gas-efficient registry
commitments, and separates deliberately designed synthetic fraud scenarios. It
also exposes important limitations that improve the credibility of the result:
branch coverage remains lower than other coverage measures, contract-size
headroom remains narrow, fraud metrics are based on a small deterministic dataset,
high-concurrency results are affected by local nonce handling, and the auditor
study has no data. The defensible conclusion is that Block-Insure is a working
and reproducible research prototype with clearly identified paths toward
production-grade decentralization, privacy, validation, and deployment.
