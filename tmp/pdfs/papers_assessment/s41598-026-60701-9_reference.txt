Abstract
Healthcare insurance fraud causes substantial financial losses, operational inefficiencies,
and reduced trust among patients, providers, and insurers. Conventional fraud detection
approaches often rely on centralized infrastructures and opaque machine learning models,
limiting transparency, adaptability, and data integrity. To address these challenges, this
paper proposes a secure and explainable healthcare fraud detection framework that
integrates blockchain technology with machine learning. A permissioned blockchain
provides decentralized, tamper-resistant storage and an immutable audit trail for claim-
related evidence, while a stacking ensemble architecture based on LightGBM and XGBoost

S identifies suspicious provider behavior from healthcare claims data. Experiments conducted
ES on a real-world dataset comprising more than 500,000 outpatient claims, 40,000 inpatient
PR claims, and 138,000 beneficiary records demonstrate effective performance under severe

class imbalance. Given the imbalanced nature of fraud detection, Average Precision (AP) was

IN adopted as the primary evaluation metric. Among the evaluated models, the Voting classifier
E achieved the highest AP score of 0.746, indicating the strongest precision–recall balance for
CL identifying fraudulent providers. The proposed stacking framework achieved competitive
TI overall performance with an accuracy of 0.940, AUC of 0.951, AP of 0.733, precision of 0.702,
AR recall of 0.630, and F1-score of 0.664. Model predictions are further interpreted using SHAP

(SHapley Additive exPlanations), providing stable feature-level explanations across multiple
validation runs. The blockchain layer securely anchors hashed prediction artifacts and
explanation records with minimal latency, enabling verifiable and tamper-resistant auditing.
These findings demonstrate the potential of integrating explainable machine learning with
blockchain-based auditability to support trustworthy healthcare fraud detection.

Keywords: Healthcare fraud detection; Blockchain; Explainable AI; Ensemble learning;
Claims analytics; Immutable audit trail; Machine learning

1. Introduction

Healthcare is a complex multidisciplinary system and is related to the prevention, diagnosis,
treatment, and management of diseases. It involves interdependent relationships among
patients, providers, organizations, technologies, and regulatory bodies, and is dependent on
                               ACCAERPTTICEDLEMIANNPURSECSRSIPT

digital information systems for managing patient records, aiding decision making, and
improving care quality. With the increase in the volume and complexity of health data, the
problems associated with the data security, interoperability, transparency, and trust have
become more acute, highlighting the need for robust technological solutions [1]. The growing
amount and volume and diversity of data produced by contemporary distributed systems
further exacerbate the difficulties in maintaining transparency and interpretability. The
cloud-native systems demand highly sophisticated anomaly detection systems, that not only
identify irregularities but also provide clear and explainable diagnostics of the system
monitoring and compliance of large-scale telemetry data. The usefulness of jointly using
scalable data processing with explainable AI methods to process high-dimensional telemetry
data was demonstrated by the work presented in the paper Explainable Anomaly Detection
in Cloud-Native Systems via RAG-Enhanced Vector Retrieval [2] which is more recent. These
challenges are seen with both blockchain-based healthcare systems where large volumes of
transactional and audit data need to be analyzed to detect fraud, and transparency and
compliance with regulations. In the healthcare environment, blockchain technology has

S emerged as a promising approach to address several of these challenges.
S Blockchain technology originally emerged as the foundational infrastructure for
RE cryptocurrencies such as Bitcoin. The technology uses a decentralized distributed ledger that
P is distributed and decentralized. This ledger keeps records of all digital transactions in blocks
N that are cryptographically linked in a chain [3]. As blockchain data is transparent, tamper-
I resistant, and immutable, it can enhance trust within healthcare systems. Authorized users
LE can access the data simultaneously, whereby all participants agree on the validity of the data,
IC for example, exchanging events safely across the entire supply chain. Blockchain in
RT healthcare facilitates secure data sharing between stakeholders to store electronic health
A records more securely for improved data integrity and permit patients’ greater control over

their health data. To build secure, auditable, and interoperable health data systems,
blockchain also provides a foundation for developing reliable applications for lower-level
organizations [4].

Figure 1 shows the schematic block structure, which stores sequential data on blockchains
in the form of "a chain of" blocks cryptographically associated with one another. Each block,
that is block n, carries in its header some crucial metadata, such as the hash (or address) of
the previous block, the timestamp, the nonce used in consensus mechanisms like proof of
work, and a Merkle root acting as a compact cryptographic summary of all transactions
carried out within the block. The previous block’s hash creates a dependency chain across
blocks (block n−1, n, and n+1), such that tampering with any block would render invalid all
subsequent blocks. Tampering with any block breaks the cryptographic linkage between
blocks which leads to failure of the chain, which in turn compromises the integrity and
traceability of the chain. Immutability, integrity, and traceability are some of the basic
properties of blockchain which make it a secure and reliable distributed ledger [5]. The
global blockchain healthcare market is expected to grow rapidly (CAGR) of 35.08% during
the forecast period and is forecasted to rise at an annual growth rate of 9.33% during 2017-
2018 along with USD 193.43 billion of revenue by the end of 2034 [6]. Driven by
technological benefits related to secure, decentralized, and integrity-preserving record

SS management, blockchain protocols contribute to the development of tamper-resistant
E databases, which can ensure distributed ledgers of such sectors remain intact. The possible
PR cost-saving to healthcare by adopting blockchain technology costs includes minimization by
N $100 billion by the reduction of overhead costs in operations, fraud and redundant cost
I related to IT equipment and data breaches [7]. Although North America holds the largest
LE market share (43% in 2024), Asia-Pacific is likely to show the greatest growth rate, mainly
IC driven by the growing government support and investment in digital health infrastructure.
T On the flipside, some challenges pose against the adoption; the upfront costs of producing
AR and integrating blockchain for the healthcare information technology and Electronic Health

Record (EHR) systems are much higher [8,9]. In the conventional method of healthcare
claims management and fraud detection, data is stored and processed in centralized
information systems that are managed by insurance providers, third-party administrators or
healthcare institutions. Fraud detection is mostly based on rule-based approaches, statistical
thresholds and traditional machine learning models that are trained using historical claims
data. These tools are used to detect trends such as unusual billing frequencies, duplicate
claims, and extended treatment cost. While these methods have achieved much of a general
acceptance, they do have limitations in terms of the transfer of information between different
stakeholders as they are limited in three keyways - privacy concerns, lack of interoperability
and regulation [10,11]. Further, these centralized systems are not only subject to single point
failures, tampering of data, or unauthorized access that may compromise the integrity of the
data and trust between participants. With advances in technology, efficient fraud detection
                               ACCAERPTTICEDLEMIANNPURSECSRSIPT

has become quite a challenge. Rule-based systems of fraud detection happen to be very rigid
and find it almost impossible to adapt to the rapidly changing and dynamic strategies of
fraud. Consequently, the rule-based systems usually find themselves with high false-positive
rates while actual instances of fraud remain unnoticed [12]. Although machine learning
models are flexible, they often rely on biased and incomplete training datasets and generally
are often perceived as black-box systems, unable to tell how the decisions are made.
Transparency is extremely important within the medical industry to ensure accountability in
regulatory compliance. Centralized repositories pose major challenges as they do not have
any guarantee of the fact that the data is confidential because of the lack of accountability
and traceability mechanism. Another challenge is that data repositories delay claim
processing, hence increasing fraud occurrences, leading to a proportional growth of financial
loss and operational inefficiency [13].
To solve these issues, this work presents a secure and explainable machine learning
architecture for healthcare fraud detection in a blockchain environment. In the framework,
the explainable machine learning models are used to identify the potentially fraudulent

S providers and the SHAP (SHapley Additive exPlanations) is used to explain the reasons that
S affect each prediction. This improves the transparency of the model, improves the
RE comprehension of the fraud detection judgments and raises the confidence in the system by
P healthcare stakeholders. A permissioned blockchain is a tool that serves as a secure and
N reliable source of audit trail for fraud evidence and information provenance, thus adding
I levels of trust between the actors in the healthcare environment to guarantee fraud detection
LE results' integrity and accountability. The integration enables the secure, transparent and
IC traceable management of fraud detection results, with improved computational efficiency of
RT off-chain ML inferences.
A While there are previous research efforts that have explored either secure management of

healthcare data using blockchain or machine learning for healthcare fraud detection, there
is limited existing work on how to combine explainable AI with blockchain auditability into a
single framework for healthcare fraud detection to date. The proposed framework extends
these earlier approaches by incorporating provider-level ensemble learning, model
interpretability via SHAP, decision-threshold optimization, and evidence recording on
permissioned blockchain. The proposed architecture does not adopt blockchain as the fraud
detection mechanism, but instead uses blockchain to guarantee the integrity, provenance,
and auditability of machine learning predictions, while augmenting current, blockchain-
enabled healthcare fraud detection architectures with added explainability, trusted decision
support, and reproducible evidence of fraud.

The architecture consists of an application layer and an interface layer accessible to
healthcare providers, payers, patients, auditors, and regulatory bodies via secure APIs,
authentication services, and decentralized applications. They are submitted, validated and
adjudicated in a deterministic manner, and a permissioned blockchain with Proof of Authority

blockchain oracle to an off-chain AI/ML fraud detection module that assigns a fraud-risk
score based on the optimized decision threshold. The fraud flag and cryptographic evidence
are securely anchored on the blockchain, while sensitive patient information remains
encrypted, access-controlled, and stored off-chain.
2.1 Related Work
Research has been conducted on various aspects of using blockchain, machine learning, and
explainable AI to detect healthcare insurance fraud, with recent studies examining these
topics from several angles. In his recent work, Kapadiya et al. (2022) [14] introduced a
blockchain-based AI solution, where a private blockchain was used to guarantee the

SS immutability of healthcare claims and a machine learning model was used to assess the risk
E of fraud. Their research identified security, scalability and system integration issues.
PR Likewise, Amponsah et al. (2022) [15] proposed a machine learning-enabled claims validation
N system that uses blockchain-based smart contracts to enhance fraud detection transparency,
I auditability, and integrity. There have been several studies concerned with enhancing health
LE care infrastructure with blockchain. To overcome this challenge, Settipalli et al., 2023 [16]
IC created a consortium blockchain structure with off-chain analytics, which not only lessens
T on-chain storage but also boosts the integrity and traceability of healthcare transactions.
AR Amin et al. 2024 [17] proposed a multi-signature smart contract system that boosted the

security of claim authorization and minimized approval fraud by leveraging decentralized
verification methods. Recently, Lataran et al. (2024) [18] combined structured with
unstructured claim text in written claims with BERT and blockchain to enhance fraud
detection, and Islayem et al. (2025) [19] used blockchain-assisted Large Language Models
(LLMs) to analyse claim histories and identify fraudulent patterns in behaviour. In addition
to blockchain-based solutions, there have been several studies focused on enhancing the
machine learning models for fraud detection. Kotekani and Ilango (2022) [20] introduced
HEMClust that used heterogeneous ensemble learning and K-prototype clustering to boost
fraud detection in imbalanced healthcare information. Pandya et al. (2023) [21] investigated
conventional machine learning and deep learning models, and applied considerable feature
engineering and preprocessing to boost fraud prediction accuracy. To enhance the
interpretability of healthcare fraud detection, Mishra 2023 [22] explored supervised learning
                                ACCAERPTTICEDLEMIANNPURSECSRSIPT

approaches and feature importance analysis. Parthasarathi et al. [23] leveraged the power

of Bayesian optimization to boost the classification accuracy of XGBoost models on

imbalanced fraud datasets, while Olivia et al. [24] showcased the benefits of using

explainable AI methods to ensure transparency and regulatory trust in automated insurance

fraud detection systems. While these studies have greatly contributed to the improvement of

secure data management in the context of blockchain and fraud prediction through machine

learning, they tend to concentrate on one of these two aspects rather than both. There has

been limited effort in combining explainable AI, provider-level ensemble learning, optimal

threshold selection, and permissioned blockchain-based auditability in an integrated

manner. The proposed work aims to fill this gap by bringing together these complementary

components, to enhance the predictiveness, interpretability, transparency and traceability

of healthcare fraud detection. A summary of existing studies is presented in Table 1, where

the methods, main contributions and limitations are compared.

2.2 Research Gap
Although there have been major advances in healthcare fraud detection, there are still some
obstacles to the successful utilization of the existing methods. Most machine learning and
deep learning models have a high predictive power but are not interpretable, and in
regulated healthcare settings, making decisions based on their predictions is hard to defend.
Second, existing fraud detection solutions tend to be centralised, which is subject to a
multitude of vulnerabilities including data tampering, data corruption, single points of
failure, and unauthorised access. Although it can increase the integrity and traceability of
the data, blockchain's primary purpose is to provide secure data management, not to detect
intelligence fraud in a secure manner. Third, the research literature is largely restricted to
the problem of Fraud Detection, the problem of Explanation and the problem of Secure Data
                               ACCAERPTTICEDLEMIANNPURSECSRSIPT

Management, and only a handful of make a concerted effort to adjoin them. Thus, there is an
important research gap in creating an end-to-end system to merge accurate fraud detection,
explainability of decision making, secure evidence management, and verifiable auditability.
The proposed framework is designed to fill this gap by implementing an ensemble learning
approach at the provider level, explainability using SHAP, and optimization of the decision
threshold selection by using a permissioned blockchain infrastructure. It is not a means of
fraud detection on blockchain, instead it's a tamper-resistant audit layer that guarantees the
integrity, provenance, and auditability of machine learning predictions. This integrated
approach will increase the framework's predictability, transparency, and accountability,
making it more successful in preventing healthcare fraud.
3. Materials and Methodology

This part includes the data set information, the pre-processing and exploratory analysis in
the course of the model development and the introduction of blockchain that renders the
data integrity and verifiable decision-making. The proposed methodology has combined the
data validation based on blockchain with the latest models of machine learning to create a

S secure and explainable pipeline of fraud detection as shown in Figure 3. High-performance
ES classifiers like LightGBM, XGBoost and Stacking ensemble are used for capturing the
PR complex and nonlinear behaviour of fraud and still to keep the results interpretable. All

experiments have been performed on a workstation equipped with an Intel Core i7 processor

IN of 8 cores, running at a speed range between 2.6-3.0 GHz, 16-32 GB DDR 4 main memory,
E 512 GB SSD, by working with 64-bit version of Windows 10 or Ubuntu 20.04, as their
CL operating systems. The implementation was carried out using Python version 3.9.13. Data
TI preprocessing and analysis were performed using Pandas (v1.5) and NumPy (v1.23).
AR Machine learning models were developed using Scikit-learn (v1.2), XGBoost (v1.7), and

LightGBM (v3.3). Model interpretability was achieved using the SHAP library (v0.41). The
blockchain component was implemented in a permissioned environment using custom smart
contracts and a pBFT consensus mechanism. Since the proposed framework does not involve
deep learning models, frameworks such as TensorFlow or PyTorch were not used.

IN 3.2 Data Preprocessing and Visualization
E The first step in data preprocessing was to perform data cleaning to enhance data quality
L and reliability. Duplicate records were eliminated, missing values were addressed with
IC appropriate imputation or exclusion techniques, and various inpatient, outpatient,
RT beneficiary, and provider data sets were merged into a single provider-level data set. All
A numerical attributes were then "normalized" to be on the same scale, so that any differences

in size of the features did not bias the results. Class imbalance was overcome by using

SMOTE-Tomek hybrid resampling technique only on the training set. The majority-class

instances were removed using Tomek Links to eliminate overlapping majority-class

instances, while the minority-class (fraudulent) instances were synthesized using SMOTE to

create a more balanced and cleaner training set for better model learning. To provide a

leakage-free evaluation, all data-dependent processing was done only on the training

partition of each GroupKFold (k = 5) split. These operations were missing-value imputation,

feature normalization, SMOTE-Tomek resampling, feature selection, PCA based

dimensionality reduction, hyperparameter optimization, and threshold selection. All the pre-

processing parameters were calculated using the training data only and then used in the

related validation fold without re-calculation. No validation sample was used at any stage of
                               ACCAERPTTICEDLEMIANNPURSECSRSIPT

the preprocessing, model tuning or training, so a strict separation between training and
validation sample was ensured and an unbiased evaluation was provided.

The stacking architecture proposed is clearly a two-level architecture. At level 1, LightGBM
and XGBoost are trained separately as they have been proven to be good in structured

predictions to avoid overfitting and achieve unbiased learning.
Since fraud detection is performed at the provider level, standard random K-fold splitting
can introduce data leakage by allowing claims from the same provider to appear in both
training and validation folds. To overcome this problem, a group-conscious cross-validation
plan is used with the Provider ID used as the grouping variable. Specifically, GroupKFold is
employed to ensure that all claims associated with a given provider are contained entirely

S within either the training or validation set in each fold. This prevents the model from learning
S provider-specific patterns that could artificially inflate performance. In this setup, the
PRE dataset is partitioned into K folds (K=5) based on provider groups. For each iteration, the

base models are trained on K−1 folds and evaluated on the held-out fold, ensuring that

IN predictions are always generated for providers not seen during training. Consequently, the
E out-of-fold (OOF) predictions used in the stacking ensemble are generated under strict
L provider-level separation, resulting in a leakage-free and more realistic evaluation of model
IC generalization.
ART Both LightGBM and XGBoost also give resulting OOF predictions which are combined to

form the meta-feature space which is utilized in the second stage in the stacking model.
Although, stacking is a well-established ensemble learning strategy, the effectiveness of the
approach relies on the diversity and complementary behavior of the selected base models.
In the proposed framework, LightGBM and XGBoost are intentionally chosen because of their
different optimization strategies and characteristics about learning when applied to
structured healthcare claims data. LightGBM uses a leaf-wise approach to growing the tree
which makes it capable to capture complex feature interactions and works well with large-
size data, whereas XGBoost uses a level-wise approach to the tree growth with a high level
of regularization that makes it more robust to noisy and imbalanced data distributions. This
difference in inductive bias results in a difference in error in the two models [32,33].
Empirically, LightGBM will usually have higher recall (it will have a larger set of potential
fraud cases) and XGBoost will make more conservative predictions (its prediction precision
is higher). The stacking framework makes use of these complementary actions by

incorporating the output of the meta-learner, balancing thus between sensitivity and

specificity. As a result, the ensemble improves both false negative and false positive rates

than individual models, which is of special importance in healthcare fraud detection

scenarios in which misclassification has serious financial and operational impact [33]. At the

second level, a logistic regression model is chosen as meta-learner because it is simple,

powerful in terms of generalization ability, and can give well-calibrated probability outputs

which are suitable for a threshold-based fraud detection. In addition, its interpretability

matches the explainability requirements of healthcare systems, and it can be transparently

integrated with SHAP explanations. In the inference, the base models are retrained on the

whole dataset while the predictions are fed into the meta-learner to obtain the final fraud

risk score.

TICL 3.5 Smart Contract Design for Claim Adjudication
AR The system proposed is a permissioned blockchain with a consensus layer based on the
Practical Byzantine Fault Tolerance (pBFT) protocol, which only allows certain entities (such

as insurers, auditors, and regulators) to take part in transaction validation. In this context,

claim adjudication is handled by a series of deterministic smart contracts, which provides

transparency, automation and auditing features. The adjudication process includes the

following steps [37, 38, 39]:

 Claim Submission Contract: This contract provides validation of the structural integrity of

incoming claims, including schema validation, digital signature and timestamp checking.

At this point, claims that are invalid or incomplete will be rejected.

 Rule-Based Validation Contract: Basic business and medical rules are pre-defined and

used to identify simple inconsistencies like duplicate claims, invalid diagnosis–procedure

combinations, abnormal billing frequencies, etc.
                               ACCAERPTTICEDLEMIANNPURSECSRSIPT

 Fraud Scoring Oracle Interface: Verified claims are sent to an off-chain machine learning
    module. The probability score for fraud is sent from the blockchain through a secure
    oracle channel.

 Adjudication Contract: A deterministic decision logic is used according to the fraud score
    calculated. If the fraud score is below a certain threshold, the claim will automatically be
    approved, meaning it is unlikely to be a fraud. On the other hand, if the fraud score is
    above the threshold, the claim is flagged to be reviewed further, which means other
    stakeholders are going to review the claim before taking a final decision. In real-world
    scenarios, this threshold can be adjusted on a regular basis to reflect current fraud trends.
    A reasonable heuristic to balance responsiveness and stability is a fixed update interval,
    such as two weeks. Shorter intervals can result in an unstable decision boundary because
    of a transient variation of the data; longer intervals can make it harder to adapt to new
    fraud behaviors. The update frequency to the threshold, however, can be configured, and
    may be adjusted as necessary based on the observed data drift, system performance, or

3.6 Blockchain Implementation Details
                               ACCAERPTTICEDLEMIANNPURSECSRSIPT

Building on the concept of smart contract-based adjudication system, the proposed system
is based on permissioned consortium blockchain to ensure control over participation, data
privacy, and regulatory compliance from the healthcare stakeholders.
The prototype assumes a permissioned consortium blockchain composed of trusted validator
nodes representing participating healthcare organizations (e.g., hospitals, insurers, auditors,
and regulators). Because the objective of this work is to validate the proposed architecture
rather than benchmark a production deployment, the exact number of validator nodes is
intentionally left deployment-dependent and can be determined according to the governance
and operational requirements of the participating organizations.
In this architecture the blockchain layer serves as a trust preserving infrastructure for
ensuring immutability, traceability, and consensus-driven validation of all the transactions
relating to claims. To ensure a very efficient agreement amongst distributed nodes, a
Practical Byzantine Fault Tolerance (pBFT) protocol is used, that provides deterministic
finality and low-latency transactions confirmation suitable for real-time healthcare
environment. Beyond being a distributed ledger, the blockchain layer is being wrapped up

S close with machine learning elements using a structured model of interaction, dividing
S deterministic operations in the on-chain environment whilst probabilistic inferences in off-
RE chain environment. The blockchain runs deterministic logic as smart contracts which include
P verifying claims, enforcing rules and adjudication decisions, computationally intensive fraud
N detection models are executed off-chain. This separation is necessary to maintain scalability
I and performance but introduces the problem with the trust in off-chain computation. To
LE counteract this, the system uses a verifiable interaction mechanism where machine learning
IC outputs are bound to the blockchain in a cryptographic manner without the need for direct
RT execution on the blockchain. Upon receipt of a validated claim, the off-chain machine
A learning module calculates fraud risk score and its explanation. Instead of being a raw output

to be stored, the system produces a cryptographic commitment, encapsulating the prediction
the model is making, as well as its explanation and the execution context. This context
contains some important information such as model version, feature transformation pipeline,
and a hash of the input data used during inference. The resulting commitment is stored to
the blockchain, providing a tamper-proof connection between a state of the blockchain and
the computation done off-chain. This design provides a repeatable way to verify the process
so that any authorized party can rebuild the inference by using the same input and
configuration and verify that the result of computation is the same as the commitment on
chain. In this way, the blockchain does not perform the machine learning computation
directly, but it will account for it via cryptographic verification. As a result, the approach
promotes increased transparency and trust and is computationally efficient. The
communication between blockchain and machine learning constituents is made possible by
        ACCAERPTTICEDLEMIANNPURSECSRSIPT

an event-driven mechanism. Smart contracts emit events when the analysis of fraud is

necessary and this is detected by a secure oracle interface responsible for calling the off-

chain inference service. The oracle serves as a trusted boundary that ensures that all

responses are authenticated and digitally signed and tied to the original transaction. This

allows the integrity and non-repudiation of machine learning outputs before they are

committed into the blockchain. To ensure responsiveness of the system, the integration is

based on an asynchronous processing model where the validation of blockchains and the

inference on machine learning models are done in parallel and synchronized only during final

adjudication. This prevents the consensus on the blockchain from being a bottleneck to the

model execution process and enables near real-time processing of claims. Further, some

consistency checks are also added to ensure that any discrepancy between recomputed

results from stored commitments results in transaction validation, which provides security

against tampering or inconsistency in execution. Architecturally this separation of the trust

and intelligence layer, together with a good cryptographic linkage between them, is clear.

Both are important to the blockchain layer for data integrity, provenance and auditability

S and the machine learning layer for predictive functions and model explainability. Making
S opaque machine learning decisions transparent and auditable by anchoring verifiable
RE representations of machine learning outputs on the Blockchain, the system enables the
P machine learning system to be held accountable. The whole idea behind this integrated
N design is to enhance the reliability, reproducibility, and compliance with regulatory
I standards in healthcare fraud detection systems in the tested experimental context. The
LE suggested framework showed promising results but still needs to be tested on larger multi-
IC institutional datasets, on production-scale blockchain infrastructures, before being deployed
RT in the real world. The performance metrics of the blockchain layer, such as latency, storage
A requirement, computational load and scalability, are summarized in Table 6. In the case of

an "as expected deployment", it is expected that a permissioned consortium architecture

with pBFT consensus can result in lower transaction latency and higher throughput

compared to typical public blockchain systems. By storing only cryptographic hashes and

metadata on-chain, and doing machine learning inference off-chain, storage and computation

resources are minimized. The listed performance metrics are observations made at a

prototype level and property-based performance measures of permissioned blockchain

networks, not production benchmarks. Therefore, reported latency, throughput and

scalability are to be regarded as indicative of the feasibility of the system under controlled

conditions. A full characterization for different network topologies and deployment scenarios

3.7 Security and Threat Analysis

The proposed framework takes into consideration several potential security threats that may

be associated with machine learning systems that are integrated with blockchain. First,

adversarial attacks may try to change input features to evade fraud detection. This risk is
                               ACCAERPTTICEDLEMIANNPURSECSRSIPT

reduced by means of a stacked ensemble (LightGBM + XGBoost) which offers robustness,
combining different patterns of learning, plus SHAP-based explanations, which is helpful to
recognize the abnormal contribution of features. Second, the system depends on some
external oracle for off-chain inference, which creates the potential for oracle manipulation.
To counter this, all the responses from oracles are digitally signed and cryptographically
hashed, which allows the verification of the responses and prevents the tampering of the
outputs of the predictions. Third, collusion risks between stakeholders (such as insurances
or auditors) are avoided with a multi-signature consensus mechanism, enabling that no single
entity can approve or reject claims unilaterally. All the decisions are captured on the
blockchain and provide a transparent and auditable trail. Finally, to avoid data leakage, data
and model output representations are hashed, and sensitive healthcare data will be securely
stored off-chain with controlled access.
The proposed framework enhances integrity, auditability, and traceability using blockchain
technology for cryptographic anchoring, but it does not aim to offer absolute privacy or
regulatory compliance using blockchain technology. Healthcare-sensitive data is stored off-

S chain with access controlled and only cryptographic hashes and metadata are recorded on-
S chain. The operational policies and technical controls that the deploying healthcare
RE organization implements are critical to secure key management, identity management and
P regulatory compliance, including HIPAA and GDPR. The proposed framework should
N therefore be seen as a safe architectural structure to be built on by further access-control
I measures, data protection measures and organizational governance to ensure that it
LE complies with the aim of regulatory requirements.
IC The security assessment presented in this work provides a qualitative evaluation of the
RT proposed framework by identifying representative threats and the corresponding mitigation
A mechanisms. The combined use of a permissioned blockchain, PBFT consensus,

cryptographic hashing, digital signatures, Merkle-tree verification, and controlled access
strengthens resistance against common threats, including data tampering, replay attacks,
unauthorized modification, and oracle manipulation. However, a formal security proof,
penetration testing, or large-scale adversarial evaluation is beyond the scope of the present
study and has been identified as an important direction for future research.

Additionally, to support the argument for the low latency blockchain anchoring, the

performance of the permissioned blockchain network was analyzed using significant aspects

of operation, making the transaction time, throughput, and block validation time. The system

uses a Practical Byzantine Fault Tolerance (pBFT) system of consensus, which makes it

possible to reach efficient consensus among a limited number of trusted nodes. Experimental

observations suggest average transaction latency to be between 120 ms - 250 ms depending

on network load and batch size. The average time taken for the block validation is seen to be

less than 1 second, ensuring that claim related transactions are confirmed quickly.

Furthermore, the approximate throughput for the system is 800-1200 transactions per

second (TPS) under moderate load conditions. The anchoring of machine learning outputs

(such as fraud scores, SHAP explanations, and the evidence hashes) brings little overhead,

since only cryptographic hash functions are stored on-chain, while bulk data is stored off-

chain. This hybrid design affords the ability to scale while preserving data integrity and

auditability. Overall, the results confirm that the blockchain layer has appropriately low

enough latency to enable nearly real-time fraud detection workflows in healthcare systems.

5 Conclusion& Future work

IN The presented framework showcases the capability of blockchain and explainable machine
LE learning in enabling safe, transparent, and traceable healthcare fraud detection. The
IC framework combines models from ensemble learning with interpretability through SHAP and
T blockchain traceability to give a structured way of determining potentially suspicious
AR provider activity and enhance accountability throughout the decision-making process. The

evaluated healthcare claims dataset shows encouraging predictive performance in a class-
imbalanced scenario, which could pave the way for applications of the proposed approach to
assist in fraud detection in other similar scenarios. The results of this study are only
applicable for the dataset and experimental environment studied, and further validation in
independent datasets and for operation in healthcare systems is needed for broader
conclusions about generalizability or operationally deployment. However, there are certain
weaknesses to be noted. While blockchain provides added security and audibility, scalability
is still a hurdle, especially in healthcare settings with high volumes of claims that may require
near real-time processing. The permissioned architecture and the efficient consensus
mechanisms like pBFT lower the latency but system-level performance could be impacted at
high transaction volumes and with higher numbers of participating nodes. In addition, the
deployment of blockchain-based systems is complex and highly resource-intensive, requiring
                               ACCAERPTTICEDLEMIANNPURSECSRSIPT

infrastructure, the integration of the technology into the current healthcare IT systems,
policies and procedures governing access control and information privacy. The results of the
experimental evaluation reported in this study are based on a single publicly-available
dataset of healthcare fraud that might not be applicable to other healthcare systems and
claim-processing environments. While the adopted GroupKFold validation strategy and
extensive performance assessment increase the robustness of the experimental results,
further validation on other datasets from other medical institutes, insurance schemes, and
geographical regions would enhance the generalizability and applicability of the framework.
Future work will therefore involve testing the proposed framework in various simulated
healthcare fraud cases as well as a range of different clinical environments. Moreover, the
current model is based mainly on claims structured data and may not adequately represent
information from unstructured clinical records or new trends of fraudulent claims. While
SHAP enhances the interpretability of models, the trustworthiness of explanations is still
reliant on the quality of the data, feature engineering decisions, and possible biases in the
data used, which could affect predictive efficiency as well as the consistency of the

S explanations. Going forward, these limitations will be addressed by developing more
S scalable, hybrid architectures to maximize on-chain/off-chain processing. Further study will
RE focus on how to integrate real-time streaming data, validation using independent data sets,
P and how to integrate the unstructured clinical information using sophisticated deep learning
N technology. Mitigating these challenges will further validate the robustness, scalability and
I real-world viability of fraud detection systems in healthcare, which are secure and
LE explainable. Apart from this, future work will also investigate alternative class-balancing
IC strategies, such as ADASYN, Borderline-SMOTE, and cost-sensitive learning, to further
RT evaluate their impact on healthcare fraud detection performance.
