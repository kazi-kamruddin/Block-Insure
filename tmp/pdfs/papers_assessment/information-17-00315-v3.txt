                                        Abstract

                                        Healthcare fraud poses significant risks to insurance systems, undermining both financial
                                        sustainability and equitable access to care. Accurate detection of fraudulent claims is
                                        therefore critical to ensuring the integrity of healthcare insurance operations. However, the
                                        increasing sophistication of fraud techniques and limited data availability have undermined
                                        the performance of traditional detection approaches. To address these challenges, this paper
                                        proposes WCGAN-GA-RF, an integrated fraud detection framework that synergistically
                                        combines Wasserstein Conditional Generative Adversarial Network with gradient penalty
                                        (WCGAN-GP) for synthetic data generation, genetic algorithm-based feature selection (GA-
                                        RF) for dimensionality reduction, and Random Forest (RF) for classification. The proposed
                                        framework was empirically validated on a real-world dataset of 16,000 healthcare insurance
                                        claims from a Chinese healthcare technology firm, characterized by a 16:1 class imbalance
                                        ratio (5.9% fraudulent samples) and 118 original features. Using a stratified 80/20 train–test
                                        split with results averaged over five independent runs, the WCGAN-GA-RF framework
                                        achieved a precision of 96.47 ± 0.5%, a recall of 97.05 ± 0.4%, and an F1-score of 96.26 ± 0.4%.
                                        Notably, the GA-RF component achieved a 65% feature reduction (from 80 to 28 features)
                                        while maintaining competitive detection accuracy. Comparative experiments demonstrate
                                        that the proposed approach outperforms conventional oversampling methods, including
                                        Random Oversampling (ROS), Synthetic Minority Oversampling Technique (SMOTE),
                                        and Adaptive Synthetic Sampling (ADASYN), particularly in handling high-dimensional,
                                        severely imbalanced healthcare fraud data.

                                        Keywords: healthcare fraud detection; WCGAN-GP; evolutionary feature selection; class
                                        imbalance; imbalanced learning; oversampling

                                 Effective fraud detection requires sophisticated data-driven approaches capable of
                           analyzing large-scale medical data and identifying subtle fraud patterns [1]. The theo-
                           retical foundation of fraud detection draws from three interconnected research streams:
                           (1) anomaly detection theory, which posits that fraudulent transactions exhibit statistical
                           deviations from normal behavioral patterns [4]; (2) imbalanced learning theory, which
                           addresses the fundamental challenge that minority class samples (fraud) are insufficient
                           to train robust classifiers in high-dimensional spaces [5]; and (3) feature selection the-
                           ory, which establishes that discriminative feature identification is critical for separating
                           fraudulent from legitimate claims in sparse data regimes [6]. These theoretical perspectives
                           inform our methodological choices. Generative augmentation addresses the data scarcity
                           problem identified by imbalanced learning theory, while evolutionary feature selection
                           operationalizes feature selection theory to identify fraud-discriminative attributes.

                                 Researchers have developed various techniques for fraud detection, ranging from
                           rule-based systems to machine learning approaches. However, conventional detection
                           approaches face two critical challenges. First, fraud techniques have become increasingly
                           sophisticated—perpetrators continuously evolve their strategies to evade detection, em-
                           ploying complex billing schemes and collusion networks that closely mimic legitimate
                           claims. These evolving fraud patterns render rule-based systems and static models ineffec-
                           tive, as they cannot capture the dynamic and adversarial nature of fraudulent activities.
                           Second, the availability of labeled fraud data is severely limited. Fraudulent cases consti-
                           tute only a small fraction of healthcare claims (typically < 10%), resulting in extreme class
                           imbalance that biases conventional classifiers toward the majority class. Moreover, the high
                           dimensionality of claim data (often > 100 features) compounds this problem, as sparse
                           minority samples are insufficient to train robust models across the vast feature space. From
                           a theoretical standpoint, these challenges can be framed as follows: the anomaly detection
                           problem is compounded by concept drift (evolving fraud tactics), while the imbalanced
                           learning problem is exacerbated by the curse of dimensionality in high-dimensional fea-
                           ture spaces [7]. These dual challenges (sophisticated, evolving fraud tactics and limited,
                           imbalanced training data) necessitate advanced data-driven approaches capable of both
                           learning from scarce fraudulent examples and adapting to emerging fraud patterns.

                                 Advancements in machine learning and deep learning techniques offer promising
                           solutions to these challenges. Machine learning algorithms, such as the support vector
                           machine with evolutionary algorithms [8], the adaptive learning and Deep Learning ap-
                           proach with an association engine [9], GAN with XGBoost [10], graph neural networks with
                           reinforcement learning [11], and CGAN [12] have shown significant potential to improve
                           the performance of machine learning models. One of the most popular approaches in the
                           literature is GAN. GANs can generate realistic samples that conform to the distribution of
                           the original data, which allows them to excel over most algorithms, especially in detection
                           problems. However, GANs could result in mode collapse and vanishing gradients. There-
                           fore, using feature engineering can help address this issue. Crucially, existing approaches
                           typically address either data imbalance or feature selection in isolation, leaving a theoretical
                           gap on how generative augmentation and feature selection interact to jointly improve
                           classification in imbalanced, high-dimensional spaces.

                                 Motivated by this gap, we propose an integrated HFD framework that synergistically
                           combines WCGAN-GP-based synthetic data generation, GA-RF-based feature selection,
                           and ensemble classification. Our framework is grounded in the theoretical premise that
                           addressing data imbalance and dimensionality reduction simultaneously creates a virtuous
                           cycle in which high-quality synthetic samples enable more robust feature importance
                           estimation, while discriminative feature selection reduces noise in the generative training
                           process. Specifically, we investigate three key aspects: (1) Whether WCGAN-GP can

                           of fraudulent claims. (2) Whether GA-based feature selection can identify discriminative
                           feature subsets that outperform conventional dimensionality reduction methods (e.g., PCA,

                           WCGAN-GP in healthcare fraud detection. Our contributions address the theoretical
                           and practical limitations of existing approaches through three complementary innovations:

                                  propose a WCGAN-GP-based generative model to address the challenge of scarce
                                  fraudulent samples in healthcare claims data. Analytically, this contribution is sig-
                                  nificant because unlike conventional oversampling methods that perform local inter-
                                  polation between existing minority instances (e.g., SMOTE, ADASYN), WCGAN-GP

                           2. Related Work

                           2.1. Machine Learning in HFD

                                 At its core, HFD seeks to reliably distinguish deceptive transactions from legitimate
                           medical activities, a crucial step toward protecting both the financial health of insurers
                           and the quality of patient care. The healthcare landscape is a complex, interactive system
                           among patients, their providers, and payers, all having motivations and limitations of their
                           own. Finding fraudulent transactions and separating them from genuine medical actions is
                           the key to discovering healthcare fraud. A sophisticated network of clients, providers, and
                           users interact with the medical program. It is crucial to ensure the economic and individual
                           attention of healthcare care companies. Each of these teams has its own limitations and
                           arguments. Cheating does not usually look like clear bad habits in a difficult program.
                           Rather, it often hides in simple-looking records, making it difficult to find, such as trying to
                           find a needle in a haystack. Machine learning has come a long way in this regard. These
                           techniques can examine large amounts of data to uncover simple, hidden patterns that
                           people may miss when using conventional quantitative methods or hand-checking. It is
                           hardly surprising that recent studies on how to use equipment understanding to find and
                           prevent healthcare fraud have increased tremendously.

                                 Academics generally employ unsupervised and supervised learning as their main
                           methods to determine fraud. When there are unlabeled data, the unsupervised method
                           has been very valuable for detecting unusual patterns. In this area, properly established
                           methods, such as K-means clustering [13–16], Local Outlier Factor Analysis [17], and
                           Principal Component Analysis [18], have established a strong foundation. The advanced
                           types of unsupervised learning resources have recently gained more interest. These include
                           deep learning techniques, such as LSTM networks [19,20] and Graph Neural Networks [21]
                           as well as a specific neuronal network designed for operating without labeled files, such
                           as Autoencoders [22,23]. All of these approaches are becoming more adept at offering
                           increasingly sophisticated detection capabilities.

                                 On the other hand, supervised methods connect insight characteristics to known fraud
                           cases using effectively labeled data. Popular methods like Gradient-boosted Decision
                           Trees, Random Forests, and plain Decision Trees are included in this class. K-nearest
                           neighbors [24–27] and various neural network approaches, as well as more developed
                           methods like Deep Neural Networks [28], are other important supervised machine learning
                           methods. In addition to strengthening supervised learning, deep learning is continuing to
                           expand. Then, Convolutional Neural Networks [29–31] are generally employed to improve
                           the precision of the monitoring.

                                 The development of hybrid techniques that combine different methods is one of the
                           most amazing things. These combined methods may be the best course of action, according
                           to the latest research combining Autoencoders with Neural Networks [32] or incorporating
                           tree-based algorithms with other techniques [33]. They may significantly improve the
                           ability to identify fraudulent actions in a variety of situations.

                                 A significant challenge in fraud detection is the limited availability of labeled data,
                           coupled with the intrinsic class imbalance in the datasets, because there are substantially
                           more legitimate instances than fraudulent activity ones. It is difficult to effectively train
                           machine learning models because there are not enough real-world examples of fraud. In
                           addition, having enough fair divisions and a fine sample size when training these samples
                           depends on how efficient they are in the training process. Methods like WCGAN-GP and

                           feature engineering are important in overcoming these issues. These methods improve the
                           functioning of fraud detection designs by making great fake information and stability data.

                           dation, enduring challenges such as inadequate datasets and skewed class distributions
                           continue to call for more sophisticated solutions. Bringing together advanced approaches,
                           particularly synthetic data generation through techniques such as GANs and refined feature
                           engineering, appears promising to raise both the accuracy and trustworthiness of HFD.

                           2.2. Data Sampling Methods in HFD

                                 A persistent issue in the detection of healthcare fraud is the pronounced imbalance in
                           datasets, where legitimate medical claims dramatically outnumber confirmed cases of fraud.
                           Due to uneven distribution, it is hard to establish reliable machine learning models. To
                           address class imbalance, academics typically employ four strategies: (A) oversampling in
                           the minority class group, (B) undersampling to refine majority class examples, (C) adjusting
                           the weight of each instance in their loss functions, and (D) generating synthetic samples
                           using GANs. Each of these approaches has advantages and disadvantages. Generally, how
                           effective they are depending significantly on the nature of the dataset and the specific fraud
                           detection scenario at hand.

                                 (A) Oversampling: Oversampling strategies seek to correct uneven class distributions
                           by improving the presence of minority categories in training data. Random Oversam-
                           pling (ROS), one of the most common methods, simply duplicates existing minority-class
                           samples. More advanced synthetic oversampling techniques generate new instances by
                           algorithmically interpolating between observed minority samples. The Synthetic Minority
                           Oversampling Technique (SMOTE) and its variations, such as Borderline SMOTE and
                           Adaptive Synthetic Sampling, are widely used approaches by strategically interpolating
                           between existing examples [34–36]. In HFD systems, these techniques are frequently em-
                           ployed. However, it is important to note that oversampling carries certain risks. It can
                           either present unrepresentative synthetic cases or cause model overfitting, especially when
                           the created samples do not capture the real pattern in the underlying data.

                                 (B) Undersampling: Undersampling addresses class imbalance by reducing instances
                           from the majority class. While likely reducing computational costs, it may also result
                           in the loss of valuable data and compromise model generalization. Methods such as
                           BalanceCascade [37] and EasyEnsemble [38] have been introduced to address this problem.
                           Both techniques have been demonstrated as effective in preserving information while
                           reducing the majority class samples in domains such as healthcare.

                                 (C) Weighting: An alternative method for solving class imbalance is by focusing more
                           on minority class samples in the training [39–41]. This is either by adjusting the weights
                           of classes inside the loss function or by designing loss functions. Nevertheless, one of the
                           main issues is that they are sensitive to exact hyperparameter choices, which in most cases
                           require a lot of tuning. Even in scenarios of extreme imbalances, weighting approaches
                           might not be effective enough since they fail to expand the literal amount of minority
                           examples. In its turn, this has been answered by some recent research into learning in
                           transformed high-dimensional spaces, where the role of a better representation of features
                           can be utilized by allowing models to capture patterns using less data on minority classes.

                                 (D) GANs-Based Methods: GANs have been found to have potential in solving the
                           issue of class imbalance by generating synthetic samples of the minority class. In HFD,
                           GAN-based methods are used to create realistic cases of fraud to augment real-world
                           data to optimize the performance of supervised models. As an illustration, the structured
                           Conditional Tabular GAN has been used to over-sample minority classes data, which
                           causes improved results with classifiers [12]. Another method uses GANs to produce

                                                                                                                            https://doi.org/10.3390/info17040315
Information 2026, 17, 315                                                                                                                                              6 of 28

                           synthetic samples labeled with anomaly scores, which are then used to train supervised
                           detection systems [42]. Despite these advantages, GAN-based approaches face several
                           challenges in healthcare fraud detection, including (1) training instability and mode collapse
                           when modeling complex, high-dimensional tabular data; (2) difficulty in capturing the full
                           distribution of rare fraudulent patterns; and (3) risk of generating low-quality synthetic
                           samples that degrade classifier performance. These challenges motivate our adoption of
                           WCGAN-GP with Wasserstein distance and gradient penalty mechanisms.

                                 In addition to solving the issue of class imbalance, the reduction in dimensionality is a
                           key factor in improving the effectiveness and efficiency of fraud diagnosis. Methods such as
                           Principal Component Analysis (PCA), Linear Discriminant Analysis (LDA), and Quadratic
                           Discriminant Analysis (QDA) reduce the size of feature spaces and retain important patterns
                           of discrimination. The other direction of research is the choice of significant features.
                           The important indicators of fraud are located using different techniques, among which
                           are greedy search techniques and spectral-based selection techniques. Dimensionality
                           reduction can be used together with semantic embedding and other sophisticated feature
                           engineering techniques to build a more thorough analytical system that further improves
                           the performance of the models.

                           2.3. Genetic Algorithms in HFD

                                 Previous methods for accessing healthcare insurance fraud tended to involve a mixture
                           of many computational structures. One such study [43] combined a genetic algorithm
                           and a K-Nearest Neighbor (KNN) classifier to study the professional profiles of general
                           practitioners. In this approach, the genetic algorithm method was applied to identify the
                           best feature weights, which in turn improved KNN. The model is applicable in the detection
                           of healthcare fraud, as it has performed excellently in modeling activities. It developed
                           another impactful study [44] in which it proposed a fuzzy clustering method with the
                           Salp Swarm Algorithm (SSA) to calculate the best cluster centers and eliminate outliers by
                           undersampling. Through these studies, it is clear that the mixture of different approaches
                           would be a great boost in fraud detection.

                                 As the healthcare sector expanded, it faced a parallel increase in fraudulent activi-
                           ties, creating significant challenges for its healthy development. In response, academics
                           in [45] developed an improved adaptive genetic algorithm (IAGA) coupled with a back-
                           propagation neural network. This IAGA-BP framework was developed to enhance the
                           network’s starting weight options. It helps in solving well-known issues, such as con-
                           vergence to local minima, slow training speed, and sensitivity to specific data samples.
                           This process was tested more quickly and accurately than traditional genetic techniques
                           using historical automobile insurance claims as a test. It becomes more difficult to de-
                           tect fraud because of the complex relationships between doctors, patients, and compa-
                           nies in the medical industry. Another study [8] adopted a dynamic weighted search
                           space strategy based on an evolutionary algorithm, hybridized with a support vector
                           machine using a modified whale optimization algorithm (MWOA). This approach was
                           rigorously tested with several evaluation metrics. The strategy first extracted anomalies
                           from the information before using powerful tools like CATBoost (https://catboost.ai),
                           XGBoost (https://xgboost.readthedocs.io), scikit-learn (Random Forest, Decision Tree:
                           https://scikit-learn.org), and LightGBM (https://lightgbm.readthedocs.io). This improved
                           the reliability of healthcare claim predictions.

                                 A study develops a new technique known as the Deep Emended Genetic Algorithm
                           (DEGA) to combat the growing problem of fake online transactions [46]. By incorporating
                           a few key new innovations, including better ways to combine and choose data, adaptable
                           changes to various circumstances, and a system that encourages competition to find the

                                                                                                                            https://doi.org/10.3390/info17040315
Information 2026, 17, 315                                                                                       7 of 28

                           best options, this approach improves the ability to detect fraud. Working in tandem with
                           DEGA, a classification system that employs dual sigmoid loss functions to better manage
                           datasets with significantly imbalanced labels.

                                 While these prior studies demonstrate the potential of genetic algorithms in fraud
                           detection, they primarily employ GA for hyperparameter tuning or hybrid model opti-
                           mization. In contrast, our proposed GA-RF method leverages evolutionary optimization
                           specifically for feature subset selection in severely imbalanced, high-dimensional healthcare
                           datasets. Unlike wrapper methods that are computationally prohibitive for large feature
                           spaces, GA-RF directly optimizes for classification performance while efficiently exploring
                           the combinatorial feature space. This approach is particularly suited for healthcare fraud
                           detection where (1) the minority class (fraudulent claims) is extremely sparse, requiring
                           discriminative feature selection to improve signal-to-noise ratio; and (2) nonlinear fea-
                           ture interactions are prevalent, necessitating a search strategy that can capture complex
                           dependencies beyond simple correlation measures.

                           3. Preliminaries

                                 This section establishes the theoretical foundations for our proposed framework. We
                           present the mathematical background of generative adversarial networks and their variants,
                           with specific emphasis on how these techniques address the challenges of healthcare fraud
                           detection, including (1) severe class imbalance where fraudulent claims constitute less
                           than 1% of all claims; (2) high-dimensional sparse feature spaces arising from complex
                           medical coding systems; and (3) the need for generating realistic synthetic fraud samples
                           that preserve the statistical properties of genuine fraud patterns. Understanding these
                           theoretical underpinnings is essential for appreciating the design choices in our WCGAN-
                           GA-RF framework presented in Section 4.

                           3.1. Generative Adversarial Networks

                                 GANs [47] have attracted a lot of interest because they have been successful in a
                           variety of fields and are a powerful tool for deep generative modeling. The core idea
                           revolves around a competitive training paradigm between two neural networks: the
                           generator G and the discriminator D. Here, G takes random noise z, which is usually
                           sampled from a straightforward distribution Pz, such as uniform or Gaussian, and produces
                           synthetic outputs G(z) designed to resemble samples from the true data distribution Pdata.
                           Meanwhile, D acts as a critic, learning to differentiate actual data examples x from those
                           produced by G. This adversarial process is captured in the following mini-max objective:

                           min  max  L(D,  G)  =  Ex∼Pdata (x) [log  D(x)]  +  Ez∼Pz (z) [log(1  −  D(G(z)))].

                             G    D

                           When the discriminator is trained to maximize the following expression,

                                                  −Ex∼Pdata(x)[log D(x)] − Ez∼Pz(z)[log(1 − D(G(z)))],

                           the generator is actually trying to reduce the Jensen–Shannon divergence between the
                           real data distribution Pdata and its own generated data distribution Pdata. The JSD offers a
                           symmetric and interpretable measure of how close two distributions are, forming a key
                           part of the theoretical basis for GANs.

                                 The main weakness of the original GAN architecture [47] is that it does not know what
                           kind of data it generates in particular categories. This is especially important when the
                           data are highly imbalanced, in which case more examples of under-represented classes will
                           be needed. Conventional GANs have a silent and strong memory of complicated patterns
                           of data, yet they cannot guide the generation procedure to specific classes.

                                                                               https://doi.org/10.3390/info17040315
Information 2026, 17, 315                                                                                            8 of 28

                                 Due to this drawback, CGANs were created, which add instructions to the generation
                           process. The key innovation is to provide each network with additional information,
                           usually in the form of category labels y. The discriminator does two functions. It determines
                           whether the samples belong to the requested group and whether the samples are realistic.
                           This refined training becomes familiar to the generator in which he creates samples that are
                           capable of fitting specific categories. The ability provides CGANs with a special potential
                           to solve the problem of data imbalance using focused data augmentation [48]. CGAN is
                           based on the original GAN in that it retains a similar dynamic training architecture but
                           incorporates conditional components. The mathematical formulation is the following:

                           min  max  L(D,  G)  =  Ex∼Pdata (x) [log  D(x|y)]  +    Ez∼Pz (z) [log(1  −  D(G(z|y)))]

                             G    D

                           where the vertical bar | denotes conditioning, meaning the discriminator evaluates x given
                           the class label y, and the generator produces samples conditioned on y. This conditioning
                           on y enables controlled generation. In practice, this means that the model not only gains the
                           ability to produce samples of desired types, but also benefits from more reliable training
                           dynamics compared to standard GANs [49].

                           3.2. Wasserstein GANs with Gradient Penalty

                                 While the original GAN has been around for some time, it continues to face challenges
                           that limit its unstable gradients and mode collapse. The study [50] suggests that many of
                           these issues originate from the use of JSD to measure the similarity between distributions.
                           One particular weakness appears when the actual and generated distributions do not overlap.
                           In this case, the JSD stays fixed at log 2, causing the generator’s gradients to disappear. As
                           a result, the generator stops receiving useful feedback and cannot be improved through
                           training. At the same time, the generator’s loss function is often influenced by what is known
                           as reverse KL divergence, pushing it toward what [51] refers to as “safe” sampling behavior.
                           This tendency causes the model to generate only a narrow set of plausible outputs rather than
                           exploring the full variety of the target data, which is referred to as mode collapse.

                                 WGAN addresses these challenges by optimizing the Earth-Mover (EM) distance
                           (also called the Wasserstein-1 distance) instead of the JSD. When using the EM distance,
                           the distance between a given distribution and Pdata can be calculated even if neither of
                           them has any overlap with the other. Therefore, the problems mentioned above can be
                           addressed. Although calculating the EM distance is troublesome in real-world applica-
                           tions, the study [50] proposed that the EM distance can be approximated by changing the
                           objective of GAN to Equation (1) as long as D is a k-Lipschitz function. This constraint
                           can be enforced by weight clipping, which limits the weights of D to a compact interval
                           [−0.01, 0.01]:

                                min  max       L(D,  G)  =  Ex∼Pdata (x) [ D( x)]  −  Ez∼Pz (z) [ D(G(z))].          (1)

                                  G    D

                                 Although WGANs marked clear progress in the development of generative mod-
                           els, researchers soon observed practical drawbacks in their use of weight clipping. The
                           study [52] indicates that this technique tends to limit what the discriminator can learn and
                           may still cause the gradients to become negligibly small during training. In response, they
                           designed WGAN-GP, a revised version that imposes a gradient penalty instead of reducing
                           the weights of the network.

                                 Intuitively, the gradient penalty works as follows: to ensure stable training, the
                           discriminator’s gradients should remain well-behaved (specifically, the norm should be
                           close to one). If the gradients become too large or too small, the training becomes unstable.
                           The gradient penalty term measures how far the gradient norm deviates from one and

                                                                                      https://doi.org/10.3390/info17040315
Information 2026, 17, 315                                                                          9 of 28

                           adds a penalty to the loss function accordingly. This is analogous to adding a “smoothing”
                           constraint that prevents the discriminator from changing too abruptly.

                                 The selection of WGAN-GP over other GAN variants is motivated by three critical
                           requirements in HFD: (1) Training stability: Healthcare claim data exhibit high dimen-
                           sionality (80 features in our dataset) and severe imbalance (fraud rate < 1%), which
                           exacerbate the training instability of standard GANs. WGAN-GP’s Wasserstein distance
                           provides more stable gradients during training, preventing mode collapse where the gen-
                           erator produces limited diversity in synthetic fraud samples. (2) Sample quality: The
                           gradient penalty mechanism ensures that synthetic fraud samples maintain statistical
                           similarity to genuine fraud patterns, which is essential for effective downstream classi-

                                 This subsection articulates the theoretical framework explaining how the three compo-
                           nents of our WCGAN-GA-RF framework—generative augmentation (WCGAN-GP), evolu-
                           tionary feature selection (GA-RF), and ensemble classification (RF)—interact synergistically
                           to address the challenges of imbalanced, high-dimensional healthcare fraud detection. We
                           formalize this interaction through testable hypotheses grounded in learning theory.

                                 The three components form a cascading pipeline with bidirectional benefits:

                           1. WCGAN-GP → GA-RF: Generative augmentation creates a more balanced training
                                  distribution, enabling more robust estimation of feature importance. In severely
                                  imbalanced settings (<6% fraud rate), standard feature importance measures are
                                  biased toward features that discriminate the majority class. By augmenting minority
                                  class samples, WCGAN-GP allows the Random Forest fitness evaluator in GA-RF to
                                  identify features that are genuinely discriminative for fraud detection, rather than
                                  features that merely distinguish the abundant legitimate claims.

                           2. GA-RF → RF Classifier: Feature selection reduces noise and computational burden
                                  while preserving discriminative power. The selected 28-feature subset captures the
                                  essential fraud patterns identified through evolutionary search, enabling the Random
                                  Forest classifier to train more efficiently and generalize better to unseen fraud cases.

                                  indirectly validated through the downstream classification performance. If synthetic
                                  samples introduce artifacts or noise, GA-RF will struggle to identify consistent dis-
                                  criminative features, and the final RF classifier will exhibit degraded performance on
                                  the test set. This implicit feedback mechanism provides a quality control signal for
                                  the generative process.

                                 between samples.

                                 Prediction: The feature subset selected by GA-RF on WCGAN-GP-augmented data
                                 have higher classification accuracy using fewer features than individual applications

                                 tive than ablated systems in which various components are ablated or substituted,
                                 demonstrating the critical nature of the synergistic combination.

                           4.3. Evolutionary Feature Selection

                                 Selection and refinement of the input variables are crucial in determining the predic-
                           tive accuracy and computational efficiency of both machine learning and deep learning
                           frameworks. The inclusion of extraneous or extensive feature collections not only imposes
                           unnecessary computational burdens, but also potentially degrades model performance.
                           In order to extract and preserve a collection of impartial factors that have a significant
                           statistical connection with the goal measure, dimensionality reduction techniques are used.
                           These optimized feature subsets later form the foundation for model development. Inter-
                           dependent variables, which provide useless information to the method, introduce noise,
                           increase overfitting tendencies, and produce less accurate predictive models. Eliminat-
                           ing features with negligible explanatory power yields multiple benefits, encompassing
                           improved model transparency, enhanced data representation capabilities, and optimized
                           predictive performance. In addition, this scheme makes it easier to make reduced models
                           with better operational effectiveness.

                                 Various dimensionality reduction techniques have been developed, encompassing
                           Principal Component Analysis, t-distributed Stochastic Neighbor integration, Linear Dis-
                           criminant Analysis, and Wrapper-based methods. GA demonstrate superior characteristics
                           to Principal Component Analysis and Autoencoder-based approaches in feature selection,
                           particularly in their capacity to process nonlinear relationships, evaluate feature importance,
                           preserve interpretability, and exhibit resilience to noisy data and anomalous observations.
                           GA-based feature selection implements an evolutionary optimization process that system-

                                                                                 https://doi.org/10.3390/info17040315
Information 2026, 17, 315                                             16 of 28

                           atically explores the feature space, terminating when convergence criteria are satisfied or
                           the optimization metric reaches a plateau.

                                 We propose the GA-RF method for feature selection, which combines evolutionary
                           optimization with ensemble classification to identify and preserve the most discriminative
                           features. Unlike filter-based methods that rely on statistical measures or wrapper methods
                           that are computationally prohibitive for high-dimensional data, GA-RF directly optimizes
                           for classification performance while efficiently exploring the combinatorial feature space.

                                 The GA-based feature selection is defined as a binary optimization problem in which a
                           chromosome represents a candidate feature subset. Specifically, a chromosome is encoded
                           as a binary vector b = [b1, b2, . . . , bn] ∈ {0, 1}n, where bi = 1 indicates that the i-th feature is
                           selected and bi = 0 indicates exclusion. The fitness function is precisely defined as follows:

                           Fitness(b) = α · F1RF(b) + β ·  1  −  |b|  (5)
                                                                  n

                           where F1RF(b) denotes the F1-score achieved by a Random Forest classifier trained on the
                           feature subset encoded by b, |b| = ∑in=1 bi is the number of selected features, n = 118 is
                           the total number of features in the original dataset.

                                 Theoretical justification for the fitness function design:

                           • F1-score term (α · F1RF(b)): We use F1-score rather than accuracy because (1) in imbal-
                                 anced fraud detection datasets, accuracy is misleading—the majority class (legitimate
                                 claims) dominates, making a trivial “all legitimate” classifier achieve >94% accuracy
                                 and (2) F1-score balances precision (avoiding false positives that accuse legitimate
                                 providers) and recall (catching actual fraud), which is essential for healthcare fraud
                                 detection where both types of errors have significant costs.

                           • Sparsity term (β · (1 − |b|/n)): Encourages compact feature subsets to improve general-
                                 ization and interpretability.

                           • Theoretical motivation: The curse of dimensionality states that as feature count increases,
                                 the data becomes increasingly sparse, requiring exponentially more samples to achieve
                                 reliable estimates. For our 16:1 imbalanced dataset with limited fraud samples, reducing
                                 from 118 to 28 features significantly improves the sample-to-feature ratio.

                           • Weight selection (α = 0.9, β = 0.1): We prioritize classification performance while still
                                 providing sufficient pressure for feature reduction. This asymmetric weighting reflects
                                 the practical reality that detection accuracy is paramount, but model interpretability
                                 and computational efficiency are also valuable for real-world deployment.

                                 The GA-RF algorithm proceeds through the following steps: (1) Initialization: We
                           generate an initial population of size P = 50 chromosomes, with each feature selected
                           randomly with probability 0.5; (2) Evaluation: The fitness of each chromosome is calcu-
                           lated using Equation (5) through 5-fold cross-validation on the training set; (3) Selection:
                           We use tournament selection with tournament size three to select parent chromosomes;
                           (4) Crossover: Uniform crossover is performed with probability pc = 0.8 to produce off-
                           spring; (5) Mutation: Bit-flip mutation is applied with probability pm = 0.01 per gene;
                           and (6) Replacement: The population is replaced with offspring while preserving the top
                           10% elite individuals. This process runs for a maximum of G = 100 generations or until
                           convergence, defined as no improvement in the best fitness for 20 consecutive generations.

                                          The dataset is divided into two categories: legitimate samples (denoted as +) and fraud-
                                    ulent samples (denoted as −). The occurrence rates for legitimate samples are 94.1% (15,056),
                                    while fraudulent samples have an occurrence rate of 5.9% (944). Thus, a binary classification
                                    task with an imbalance ratio of 16:1 is defined as the fraud forecast problem. According to this
                                    ratio, there are approximately 16 legitimate examples for every fraudulent record.

                                          To ensure rigorous and reproducible evaluation, we employ a stratified train-test
                                    split with the following protocol: (1) Stratified sampling: The dataset is partitioned using
                                    stratified sampling to preserve the class distribution ratio in both training and test sets,
                                    ensuring that the minority class is adequately represented in the test set; (2) Random seed:
                                    We set a fixed random seed (seed = 42) for reproducibility; (3) Data leakage prevention:
                                    The test set is held out completely during model training, hyperparameter tuning, and
                                    feature selection. All data augmentation (via WCGAN-GP) and feature selection (via GA-
                                    RF) are performed exclusively on the training set; (4) Split ratio: The empirical framework
                                    allocates 80% of the dataset for model training and reserves 20% for evaluation purposes;
                                    and (5) Multiple runs: To assess performance variability, we report results averaged over
                                    five independent runs with different random seeds (seeds ∈ {42, 123, 456, 789, 1024}), with
                                    standard deviations reported in all tables. We note that while k-fold cross-validation is
                                    commonly used for model selection, we employ hold-out validation in this study because
                                    (a) the dataset size (n = 16,000) provides sufficient test samples for reliable evaluation;
                                    (b) the severe class imbalance (16:1) makes stratified hold-out more practical than k-fold
                                    for ensuring minority class representation in each fold; and (c) the computational cost of
                                    training WCGAN-GP makes repeated k-fold training prohibitive.

                                          We performed preprocessing activities to ensure correct data analysis and preparation
                                    for further analysis. The dataset was optimized to identify significant factors in detect-
                                    ing fraud. Statistics are carefully structured and measured by category, following the
                                    classification of the data into training and testing subsets.

                                          The experimental data were divided into two groups, that is, training and testing,
                                    using machine learning protocols, which used a split percentage of 8:2. The imbalance
                                    in distribution among classes was addressed using four different oversampling methods
                                    that include ROS, SMOTE, ADASYN, and our WCGAN-GP. The WCGAN-GP approach,

                                 Figure 5 shows the evolution of the loss during WCGAN-GP training, exhibit-
                           ing a period of initial divergence followed by stable convergence after approximately
                           10,000 iterations. To quantitatively assess the training dynamics, we analyzed several key
                           metrics: (1) Convergence speed: Both generator and discriminator losses stabilized within
                           10,000 iterations, demonstrating efficient training; (2) Loss stability: After convergence, the
                           generator loss maintained a low variance of 0.15 ± 0.02, while the discriminator loss stabi-
                           lized at 0.85 ± 0.03; and (3) Loss ratio: The generator-to-discriminator loss ratio converged
                           to 0.18 ± 0.01, indicating a balanced adversarial dynamic. These quantitative indicators
                           confirm that the model achieved a stable equilibrium, with neither component dominating
                           the training process.

                           10,000 iterations, as shown in Figure 5, demonstrates successful model training. A trained
                           GAN is considered successful when the generator loss plateaus below 0.2 and the discrimi-
                           nator loss plateaus above 0.8, as these values represent a balanced adversarial interaction
                           where neither component dominates. This equilibrium enables the GAN to produce highly
                           realistic synthetic samples while maintaining robust discrimination capabilities.

                                 The superior performance of WCGAN-GP over traditional oversampling methods
                           can be attributed to fundamental differences in synthetic sample generation. SMOTE and
                           ADASYN rely on linear interpolation between minority class samples, which limits their
                           ability to capture complex, nonlinear relationships in high-dimensional feature spaces
                           (118 features in our dataset). This approach becomes increasingly problematic in severely
                           imbalanced scenarios (16:1 ratio) where the minority class occupies a sparse region of the
                           feature space. Linear interpolation may generate synthetic samples that fall outside the
                           true data distribution, introducing noise and reducing model performance. In contrast,
                           WCGAN-GP learns the underlying data distribution through adversarial training, enabling
                           it to generate diverse and realistic samples that better represent the complexity of fraud-
                           ulent claims. The Wasserstein distance provides stable gradients even when the real and
                           generated distributions have disjointed supports, while the gradient penalty ensures the
                           discriminator satisfies the Lipschitz constraint, leading to more stable training dynamics.
                           This theoretical foundation enables WCGAN-GP to produce high-quality synthetic samples
                           that effectively augment the minority class without introducing artifacts.

                                 A notable observation from Table 3 is the precision–recall trade-off for the minority
                           class: WCGAN-GP achieves the highest precision (88.62 ± 1.2%) but a relatively lower
                           recall (37.64 ± 1.5%) compared to ADASYN (51.97 ± 1.9%). This trade-off arises because
                           WCGAN-GP generates high-quality synthetic samples that lead to a more conservative
                           classifier—while it identifies fraud cases with high confidence (evidenced by the high
                           precision), it may miss some genuine fraud cases. Despite the lower recall, WCGAN-GP
                           still achieves the best F1-score (52.61 ± 0.9%) among all methods, indicating an overall
                           better balance. Furthermore, in healthcare fraud detection, high precision is often more
                           desirable than high recall because false positives (incorrectly flagging legitimate claims as
                           fraudulent) can erode trust and cause unnecessary investigations. The 88.62% precision
                           means that 88.62% of claims flagged as fraudulent are truly fraudulent, reducing the burden
                           of manual review on legitimate claims.

                                 The computational costs of the proposed framework are as follows: (1) WCGAN-
                           GP training: O(n · d · I) where n = 12,800 training samples, d = 80 features, and
                           I = 10,000 iterations. On our hardware (NVIDIA RTX 3080 GPU), WCGAN-GP training
                           takes approximately 15 min. (2) GA-RF feature selection: O(P · G · k · RFtrain) where
                           P = 50 population size, G = 100 generations, and k-fold cross-validation (k = 5). This
                           takes approximately 8 min on a standard CPU. (3) RF classification: Training takes
                           O(n · dselected · t · log t) where dselected = 28 features and t = 100 trees, requiring less
                           than 1 min. The total training pipeline completes in under 30 min, making it practical for
                           periodic model updates in real-world deployment scenarios.

                           6. Conclusions

                                 Due to its complex systems and huge financial moves, the healthcare industry has
                           emerged as a premier destination for fraud activities. The changing demography, including
                           an aging population, offers these insurance programs even more money, adding even more
                           stress to their balance. Fraud eventually threatens both the economic stability and the
                           healthcare of the individuals who rely on these services. Advances in machine learning,
                           improved data collection techniques, and the expansion of public state data all contribute to
                           the development of new approaches to fight back. With these tools, fraud can be identified
                           more complex and powerfully through advanced analyses. It has become so important to
                           develop powerful computerized systems that identify suspicious trends and contact related
                           functions right away.

                                 Severe class imbalance, high-dimensional feature spaces, and multiple-class distribu-
                           tions are common hallmarks of HFD datasets. Conventional HFD models trained on such
                           imbalanced datasets generally exhibit exceptional classification accuracy for majority-class
                           samples but underperform in minority-class instances. In addition, the feature selection
                           process poses a major problem because it affects both the accuracy of the monitoring and
                           the computational efficiency. We present a novel framework that combines WCGAN-GP
                           with GA features optimization and prediction modules to target these limitations. The
                           WCGAN-GP component helps to oversample the minority samples in an efficient manner,
                           and the procedure of the GA-based feature selection process meticulously removes the
                           redundant features, and a closing classification structure is pursued. Experimental findings
                           indicate that the proposed design drastically beats current techniques in correcting class
                           imbalance problems in healthcare claim information. Our feature selection strategy, which
                           is based on GA, has superior measures of efficiency. The proposed HFD system is effective
                           in detecting fraud within the system due to the built-in architecture.

                                 Future research directions refer to various interesting directions that can be taken
                           to improve the proposed framework further. To begin with, the development of deep
                           learning classifiers is a natural continuation of this project. In particular, we will explore
                           (1) transformer-based architectures to discover long-range dependency in sequential

                                                                                         https://doi.org/10.3390/info17040315
Information 2026, 17, 315                                                                                                                                             25 of 28

                           healthcare claim data, which could enhance the ability to detect complex fraud patterns
                           across the dimension of time; (2) Graph Neural Networks (GNNs) to discover relationships
                           among healthcare providers, patients, and claims, which could be used to detect network-
                           based fraud schemes; and (3) hybrid deep learning models to combine the representational
                           power of neural networks and the interpretability of tree-based models. These deep learning
                           methods may substitute the existing Random Forest classifier in the WCGAN-GA-RF
                           structure and would possibly enhance detection accuracy without reducing interpretability
                           in attention mechanisms and feature attribution techniques.

                                 Second, the domain of advanced optimization methods has great potential in en-
                           hancing the computational effectiveness and the performance of the model. We suggest
                           investigating (1) Bayesian optimization for hyperparameter tuning of WCGAN-GP to
                           replace the current grid search approach, which, as a probabilistic optimization method,
                           can reduce training time while identifying optimal configurations; (2) multi-objective
                           evolutionary algorithms (e.g., NSGA-II, MOEA/D) for feature selection, enabling simul-
                           taneous optimization of multiple criteria such as classification accuracy, feature sparsity,
                           and computational cost; and (3) adaptive learning rate schedulers and neural architecture
                           search algorithms to automatically optimize the network structure of the generator and
                           discriminator. These optimization measures may minimize the computational overhead of
                           the framework while maintaining or improving detection performance.

                                 Third, future work will be concerned with practical deployment considerations. This
                           involves the creation of explainable AI (XAI) techniques to obtain interpretable fraud
                           detection results for regulatory compliance and auditor review, implementing real-time
                           streaming architectures to generate low-latency results for incoming claims, and exploring
                           federated learning methods to train models across various healthcare organizations while
                           maintaining data privacy. Additionally, integrating multi-modal data sources (e.g., medical
                           images, clinical notes, temporal claim sequences) could further enhance the model’s ability
                           to detect advanced fraud schemes that would evade detection when working with claims
                           data alone.

                                 Despite the promising results, this study has several limitations that warrant acknowl-
                           edgement. First, the empirical validation is conducted on a single healthcare claims dataset
                           from one region (China), and generalization to other healthcare systems with different
                           fraud patterns and regulatory environments requires further investigation. Second, the
                           framework assumes that labeled fraud data is available for training, which may not hold
                           in deployment scenarios where fraud labels are scarce or unreliable. Third, while our
                           approach achieves high detection accuracy, the real-world economic impact and return-
                           on-investment of deploying this system remain to be validated through operational pilots.
                           Finally, regulatory and privacy considerations, particularly regarding the use of synthetic
                           patient data and cross-institutional model deployment, are important practical concerns
                           that are beyond the scope of this methodological study but essential for eventual adoption.
